## Method and interpretation

The unit of evidence is a convention in a dated or date-bounded source. When available, a diplomatic edition, visible imprint or archive transcript supplies the spelling. Handbooks support broader statements about frequency or distribution. Repeated examples from a single source show variation within that source and do not carry independent weight in the period ratings. The set privileges Roman epigraphy, English, Scottish and French records, Iberian inscriptions and early printing. It cannot establish frequencies for all documents across a period. Where a transcript flattens spacing or uses Unicode for a historical form, the example explains that choice.

The score conditions on the relevant type of source. **0** means unlikely as a software prior or currently unsupported, **1** means rare, genre-limited or provisionally reported, **2** means attested with usable evidence, and **3** means typical within the documented genre or tradition. Zeros are not evidence of absence. The vectors are ordinal editorial scores. Each row marks whether the existing vector should be retained or revised, or has insufficient support for the rule. A conditional feature such as `clockIIII` applies when the user is reading a clock.

The period is the date the numeral was written, where known. An artefact may refer to a different year, as the Statue of Liberty tablet does. `values` gives the documented intended value, in the form of a civil year when an era or omitted-century tag specifies one, or a raw number where a regnal or indiction interval needs more inputs. `why` states the distinction for such cases. Some records preserve the excerpt as transcribed by a specialist or publisher. `read_full_text` is true only for records inspected in full. False includes books or articles consulted by chapter, search extracts, illustrations and catalogue-only access, with the specific material in `pages`. The sources include historical books digitised on Wikimedia and Wikisource.

## Existing conventions and chronograms

### Standard form — `standard`

Egbert distinguishes the usual additive epigraphic practice from subtractive alternatives, and RIB 2205 writes XLI beside four old thousand signs in 142–180 CE. At Rome the 1499 Knyght epitaph still uses an irregular pair, while early modern Köbel used raised multipliers for arithmetic. A sequence that follows the modern six-pair grammar is historically possible, but it has little dating power. The present implementation also accepts some noncanonical strings because its tests are local rather than a canonical round trip. ### Additive fours and nines — `additive4`

The 121 CE Hadrian coin has DCCCLXXIIII and the Diocles inscription (146–150) has XXXXII. The three Roman Christian dates 963, 977 and 981 use DCCCC. The 1493 Milan Rithmi prints lxxxx, the 1609 John Tod inventory has Lxxxxj and Admiralty Arch has MDCCCCX (1910, completed 1912). On the Barnes clock of c. 1890–1910, the IIII is a dial convention whose frequency cannot be assigned to all modern numeral uses. Egbert's frequency claims are qualitative and weighted towards lapidary practice. ### Irregular subtraction — `irregularSub`

Classical I before L or higher is exceptional in Egbert's summary of Mommsen. The strongest reading for IC is late medieval, when Pollard documents the printers' abbreviations for the 1490s. Forcella transmits MCCCCIC from the Thomas Knyght epitaph of 1499, independently of the printing evidence. IL and XM should appear only as unverified calculated possibilities unless a source for the exact pair is supplied. An isolated IC might also belong to a different script or to an abbreviation. ### Repeated subtraction — `runSub`

CIL I 536 has CCCXXC for 380 (157 BCE as dated by Egbert), and contemporary Flavian titulature supplies IIX for eight and XIIX for eighteen. Egbert calls standalone IIX rare in inscriptions, even though efficient repeated subtraction for 80 appears more often. Pollard gives iic through iiiic for the last years of fifteenth-century imprints. Use independent tags for repeated subtraction and irregular base-pairs when both apply. ### Every letter added — `additiveSum`

Cappelli's German tabulation p. 435 has VIX as 16 and VX as 15 (14th century), in the Latin ordinal order sexto decimo and quinto decimo. The p. 433 case IIIX is glossed as 13. The English translation and Cajori's transcription have inconsistent strings or values for these examples, so consult the plate before encoding them. A Roman-numeral chronogram adds explicitly selected letters and deserves its own mode. The cited ordinal cases do not establish a period-wide rule of unordered addition. ### Superscript multiplier — `multiplicative`

Cappelli’s §7.73 describes raised C and M in late medieval writing. The 1456 plate combines a raised hundred with an ordinal marker. Köbel’s 1514 printed fraction and the May 1580 Scottish household accounts supply dated raised hundreds. Cajori's 1771 Dutch example nests a raised hundred inside the coefficient for thousands. Chrisomalis places the development of new medieval multiplicative forms in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. Exclude Ø-like marks and abbreviation strokes from a generic superscript-multiplier rule. ### Two numbers run together — `separate`

Cappelli, Pollard and the Scottish transcription guidance establish terminal j. The 1492 XCij and 1609 Lxxxxj demonstrate the long-i form. With their unit labels, two fields can be distinguished in the 1580 payment accounts. Turning an interior j into a boundary without the labels has competing explanations, including a following hundred or score. No securely attested example of a bare fused token with only j marking the boundary was found. ### Apostrophus — `apostrophus`

RIB 2205 provides four ↀ thousand signs in its diplomatic edition. Priscian's account around 500 is available through Cajori, who thinks the form's wider use may postdate Antiquity. Epigraphic sources and Keyser describe early glyphs. The extent of surviving stand-alone CIↃ use depends on which related glyphs are counted. Dorn's 1594 title-page gives both CIↃ and IↃ. These signify 1000 and 500 respectively. With two matching arcs 1000 becomes 10,000, and with three it becomes 100,000. A missing C in a scanned English translation makes the last relationship look inconsistent. Check the Italian introduction and primary forms. ### Years from the founding of Rome (AUC) — `auc`

Hadrian's 121 CE aureus gives 874 since the city's foundation. Mattingly treats the underlying Varronian epoch as 753 BCE, with a possible 21 April start for that occasion. The normal date of a classical legal instrument may instead be identified by consuls or office counts. Humanists and modern chronologists also write AUC retrospectively. For the chosen epoch use astronomical year AUC minus 753, which gives civil 753 BCE for AUC 1 and 1 CE for AUC 754. Other epochs and day-level conversion require further evidence. ### Regnal years and other reckonings

| Candidate | Evidence and behaviour |
|---|---|
| Regnal, pontifical and episcopal years | Require a named ruler or officeholder, jurisdiction and the dating authority’s start rule. ### Other numeral forms

- **Apostrophus and large signs.** The character inventory already accepts ↀ, ↁ, ↂ, ↇ, ↈ and IↃ. Give each sign a visible source-backed gloss and show the difference between a frame covering the entire glyph, a unit of thousands and a mark over part of a string. The preserved ↀ group from RIB 2205 is usable now. The 36 CE frame example is a secondary transcription that still needs checking against the inscription. [RIB 2205](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/2205) · [Perry, figs. 16–20](https://www.unicode.org/wg2/docs/n3218.pdf) · [Chrisomalis, table 4.11](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/numerical-notation/4C3107573159D539D04DD7307423FA36)
- **Final j and long i.** Use j as a documented graphical variant where palaeographic context supports it. The user should be able to enter ij and iij, with an alternative transcription for a long terminal i. A ligature and an extended last stroke both need their own evidence when they look alike. Nagl's anti-addition explanation is a possible historical motive cited by Cajori. The 1492 Beichtbuchlein and 1580 accounts supply dated readings. [Cajori §54](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/A_History_Of_Mathematical_Notations/Volume_1/Romans) · [Pollard, p. 183](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/56628/56628-h/56628-h.htm) · [National Records of Scotland, documents 3–4](https://scottishrecordsassociation.org/documents/NRS-Scottish_handwriting_self-help_kit-web.pdf)
- **u and v.** Retain the visible letter. Normalise u to v when the hand and numeral context support five. A run of strokes can also be ii. The National Archives describes position-dependent u/v forms, and Clemens and Graham describe a rounded numeral five in medieval manuscripts. [The National Archives, sample alphabets](https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/latin-palaeography-activities-and-documents.pdf) · [Clemens and Graham, ch. 6, p. 91](https://discover.library.unt.edu/catalog/b3451328)
- **XL and XXXX, clock-face IIII.** Classical RIB 2205 gives XLI, while Diocles's inscription has XXXXII in the same broad era. The 1609 Tod inventory uses XL and Lxxxx in different amounts, with terminal j in the latter. A period-only switch cannot explain this distribution. Expose the clock convention only for dials and retain monuments with deliberately additive sequences. [RIB 2205](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/2205) · [Ozcáriz Gil, p. 36](https://agora.edu.es/descarga/articulo/8065051.pdf) · [NRS, doc. 4](https://scottishrecordsassociation.org/documents/NRS-Scottish_handwriting_self-help_kit-web.pdf) · [Barnes dial](https://www.barnesfoundation.org/research-notes-clock)
- **Punctuation, ordinals and fractions.** Dots may bound a numeral or separate its positions. Strokes may distinguish figures from ordinary letters, indicate a unit or carry multiplication. Raised o belongs to inflected ordinals. The semis S and fraction strokes need a mode with fractions and explicit units if implemented. Preserve the original separators and markings. [Clemens and Graham, p. 91](https://discover.library.unt.edu/catalog/b3451328) · [Cappelli §§7.51–7.73](https://bms-du-chatillonnais.e-monsite.com/medias/files/latin-abreviations-medievales-pour-la-paleographie.pdf) · [Egbert, pp. 72–77](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/47/Introduction_to_the_study_of_Latin_inscriptions_%28IA_cu31924075494371%29.pdf)
- **Mixed notation.** The 1388 four-score phrase uses written French for its residual count. The Scots 1663 date combines part of a written year with numeral and unit abbreviations. Cappelli tabulates C4 for 104 in a fourteenth-century specimen. These are opt-in passage-reading or document profiles. A document-aware reader should identify which numbers belong to the same field, and which words supply an amount or a date. [Chrisomalis, table 4.13](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/numerical-notation/4C3107573159D539D04DD7307423FA36) · [Scotland’s People, Is it a date?](https://api.scotlandspeople.gov.uk/scottish-handwriting/coaching/problem-solver/problem-words/dates) · [Cappelli, p. 430](https://archive.org/stream/lexiconabbreviat00capp/lexiconabbreviat00capp_djvu.txt)

## Open questions

1. Verify the León epitaph against a museum record or edition and look for independently dated Latin-script examples with a securely omitted thousand and written hundreds. Seek early modern examples of a securely omitted century.
2. Count matched variants in defined corpora by century, number, place and medium before assigning frequencies. Estimate fours separately from nines and individual subtraction pairs separately from generic subtraction.
3. Check Cagnat online in another scan, Gordon’s pp. 44–47 directly, Bischoff’s p. 176 directly, Menninger’s Ostia example, Karpinski’s signboard, and the manuscript behind Cappelli’s LXL. The Cluj PDF failed. Gordon and Bischoff were consulted through citations and reproductions. Keyser was read in excerpts.
4. Inspect Cappelli’s glyphs alongside Du Cange's competing lists and original dated artefacts. OCR can turn apostrophi into digits, conflate superscript and baseline forms, or obscure distinctions between similar numeric glyphs.
5. Identify historical evidence for taking j alone as a separator or treating W as ten in a chronogram. Confirm regional variation in marking words before raising the relevant rating.
6. Establish a simple publication convention for a baseline numeral, a diplomatic string and optional user-selected context. A year and an ordinary value can agree numerically while referring to different things.
